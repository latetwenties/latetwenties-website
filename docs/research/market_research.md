## 1. Audience definition

The audience for this site is not "small business owners in NZ/AU."

It's **local service business owners who have either been burnt by a marketing agency before, or watched a peer get burnt and stopped trying as a result, yet don't have the time to figure it out themselves.** Tradies, allied health practitioners, accountants, family-run service businesses, owner-operators on the tools. People whose customers search "[service] near me" and who know intuitively that their online presence is costing them work but have stopped believing anyone can fix it. Most days, immediate money and immediate fires take priority over anything that builds pipeline.

Three behavioural sub-segments, each with a different relationship to the category:

**A. The Defected.** Tried one or two agencies. Spent money. Got nothing meaningful, or worse, got a site that didn't feel like them. Filed marketing under "too hard" and moved on. Not actively shopping. Reachable only by someone they already trust, or by a moment of realisation that breaks the resignation.

**B. The Quietly Losing.** Have never hired an agency. Don't know they're losing work. Get the occasional referral and assume that's all there is. Will become reachable the moment the screen-share moment lands (RBM and PhysioPlus pattern: "where am I on the map" leading to "we need to commit").

**C. The Wary First-Timers.** Newer business, growing through referrals, just becoming aware that they need to be findable. Heard horror stories from peers. Want to do this once, properly. Will pay a real number for the right partner if the partner doesn't sound like the partners that burnt their friends.

The site needs to land for all three. The Defected need to feel like this is different. The Quietly Losing need the moment of realisation built into the page. The Wary First-Timers need permission to trust without committing to a long contract. Most agency sites speak to none of them and instead pitch the lifestyle business owner already converted to the idea of marketing investment.

## 2. Customer language bank

The verbatim phrases that follow come from public sources or directly from Brendan's calls. Verbatim quotes are tagged with their source. Paraphrased patterns capture the shape of repeated complaints across multiple sources without lifting any single quote.

### 2.1 Questions owners are actually asking

These are surfacing across forums, in agency rebuttal content, and in Brendan's calls. The list privileges questions that recur, not questions that are merely interesting.

1. Where am I on the map for [my service] in [my suburb]?
2. Why am I getting fewer calls than [competitor up the road]?
3. How long until I see results?
4. What does the monthly fee actually cover?
5. Will you lock me into a long contract?
6. Can I see what you've done for businesses like mine?
7. How do I know if it's actually working?
8. Why do other agencies always need "more time" and "more budget"?
9. Do I really need a new website, or can you fix the one I have?
10. What happens to my Google profile and site if I stop paying you?
11. How much of my time will this take? I'm already on the tools.
12. Will you talk to me directly or hand me to an account manager?
13. Are you going to make me look like everyone else?
14. Can you get me ahead of [named competitor]?
15. Why did the last person I paid disappear after the first month?

### 2.2 Fears and objections

The owner-side fear set divides into five clusters, each well-evidenced.

**The smoke-and-mirrors fear.** Owners worry about paying for activity disguised as outcomes. The pattern across complaint surfaces: agencies producing dashboards full of impressions, traffic, keyword movements, and bounce-rate language, while the phone stays quiet. "reports filled with impressive graphs, but no new customers" captures this exactly. The Capterra piece on telling whether your agency is working underlines the same fear: that the deliverables look impressive but are disconnected from anything that pays bills. Brendan's own field-note on this: agencies pivoting to "look at all the traffic we got you" when results don't materialise.

**The lock-in fear.** Multi-month contracts that don't perform but can't be exited. Across sources, this surfaces as "we hate our marketing company but can't switch." Locking you in for a long-term contract for retainer-based services is a red flag, especially when the contract runs over a year. The fear is not just financial. It's reputational: paying someone publicly attached to your brand who isn't delivering.

**The bait-and-switch fear.** Polished sales call. Vague delivery. Brendan's direct articulation, paraphrased from his agency-side experience: a sales team makes promises, the work gets handed off to people who can't deliver them, and the response when results don't come is a deflection. The agency rebuttal pieces inadvertently confirm this fear by addressing it directly.

**The disappearing-after-the-start fear.** The retainer-specific anxiety: a great kickoff, then radio silence. Owners watching the monthly invoice come through with no visible work output. Brendan's own observation: this is structural to retainers, not a question of agency intent. Without a monthly artefact and visible cadence, even agencies doing real work trigger this fear.

**The "they'll make me look like someone else" fear.** Specific to design and web work. The architect Brendan named came to him after two agencies, one of which had tried to replicate a known competitor's site in Australia. The owner ended up feeling like a placeholder, not a client. This is a research-and-listening failure dressed up as a design failure.

### 2.3 How owners describe agencies that earned their trust

Synthesised from positive review language across NZ/AU agency surfaces and Brendan's own client testimonials.

- Got it right the first time without endless revision rounds
- Talked to me, not at me
- Showed me what was actually happening, in plain language
- The person on the call was the person doing the work
- Didn't push services I didn't need
- Felt like they cared about my business specifically
- Made me feel like I wasn't being sold to
- Came in gumboots if needed (a Tradiesocials review captured this pattern: shows up the way a tradie shows up, on the same terms)
- Treated my business with the seriousness I treat it with myself

### 2.4 How owners describe agencies that burnt them

- Talked a big game and delivered nothing
- Used words I didn't understand to hide the fact that nothing was happening
- Promised the world and delivered a template
- Disappeared after the first month
- Charged me more than I earned in new business
- Made my site look like every other site in my industry
- Wouldn't tell me what they were actually doing each month
- Tried to upsell me into ad spend when the SEO didn't work
- Held my logins hostage when I tried to leave
- Spent more on suits and offices than on the actual work

### 2.5 NZ-specific language worth using

- "On the tools" (every NZ/AU tradie understands this; signals you've sat in their chair)
- "Up the road" (better local marker than "in your area" for this audience)
- "Sorted" (Kiwi register for "fixed properly," carries weight that "solved" doesn't)
- "Ringing" (phone-ringing is the conversion metric tradies actually care about)
- "Quietly" (matches the manaakitanga register, contrasts with "dominate" / "smash")
- "Properly" (carries serious-craftsmanship signal; one of the strongest words in the bank for this brand)
- "Mate" (use sparingly, but it's the warmth signal that pure-Auckland-corporate misses)
- "Work" / "jobs" / "calls" (use these instead of "leads" wherever possible; owners think in jobs, agencies think in leads)

## 3. The defected ICP pattern (spine for the homepage)

This is the most important pattern in the entire substrate. It warrants its own section because it changes how the homepage opens.

**The architect.** Local cafe-running, part-time architect. Two previous agencies. One tried to replicate a known competitor's site from Australia. He didn't recognise himself in the result. Filed marketing under too-hard-basket, gave up. Reachable only because Brendan asked him directly, as someone he already knew, and the question arrived at a moment when his cafe customers had started telling him "we can't find you online."

**RBM Concrete.** Family connection, warm lead, but not closed until Brendan screen-shared. Searched "concreter Bairnsdale," scrolled past the map pack, found RBM at position 12. They asked about the competitor at position 1. Realised the competitor (working the same patch) was getting most of the calls. Closed on three months of Presence Care immediately, on the basis of seeing where they actually stood.

**PhysioPlus.** Different industry, identical pattern. Asked Brendan where a specific competitor was sitting. Heard "right at the top." Closed on Foundations on the basis of the diagnostic plus a measured promise.

The unifying mechanism: **invisible loss made visible.** None of these prospects bought a marketing service. They bought the moment of realisation that they were losing work to someone they could see ranking above them. The agency's job in that moment is to be the calm voice naming what's happening, not to sell the fight.

This pattern is the homepage opener. Translated as copy direction (not final wording):

- The reader arrives. The site asks them, calmly, to search their service and their suburb.
- The reader confronts where they actually rank.
- The site offers, quietly, to do something about it.
- No bully copy. No "dominate." No "outrank."
- The competitor never gets named or characterised. The competitor is just evidence of the gap.

This pattern is also why the Free Presence Check is the load-bearing CTA, not "Book a consultation." The presence check IS the screen-share moment, codified as an offer.

## 4. Gaps in the category

What owners want that the broader NZ/AU agency category does not deliver well. These are the openings the brief should make load-bearing.

**4.1 The diagnostic before the pitch.** Most agencies pitch from the moment of first contact. Owners want someone to look at where they actually stand before recommending anything. The screen-share moment that closed RBM and PhysioPlus is the structural answer to this gap. Almost no NZ agency offers a substantive free presence audit as the entry point. Most offer "free consultations" which everyone knows are sales calls.

**4.2 Pricing visibility before the call.** Agency category default is hidden pricing. The ones who do show pricing earn trust before the conversation starts. Firefly Digital's clear monthly management fee with platform spend going direct to Google or Meta is a rare positive example surfaced in NZ-specific listings. Most agencies hide pricing because they price by what they think you'll pay. Owners feel this even when they can't articulate it.

**4.3 The work-output gap on retainers.** Owners pay monthly and don't know what they're paying for. The category response is to send dashboards. The actual answer is a tangible monthly artefact: a structured email or a one-page summary or a Loom video, naming what was done, what changed, what's next. Almost no agency does this consistently. Brendan's Presence Care can lead the category by making the artefact a contractual deliverable.

**4.4 The seriousness-without-bigness gap.** Owners want their business taken seriously but don't want to deal with a big agency. They've been burnt by both the cheap end (cookie-cutter packages, no listening) and the expensive end (account managers, suits, jargon, no actual work happening). The opening is for a small, senior operation that takes the work seriously without the overhead. Latetwenties sits in this gap by definition. The site needs to make the gap visible before the prospect names it themselves.

**4.5 The senior-person-does-the-work gap.** Owners want the person who pitched them to be the person doing the work. The bait-and-switch fear surfaces in agency rebuttal content across multiple sources, and Brendan's own field notes name it directly: a sales team makes promises, the work gets handed off, results don't materialise. Most agencies can't offer continuity structurally because the operating model splits sales from delivery. Latetwenties can. The site needs to make this legible without bragging about it. Photography of Brendan working, named attribution in pull-quotes, the "the person you talk to is the person doing the work" pattern as a quiet recurring frame.

**4.6 The "I just want to be findable" gap.** Owners who aren't trying to dominate, just trying to show up. Agency category language is full of "dominate," "outrank," "smash competitors." Owners often want something quieter: "be the one they call when they search." The big-and-honest register we landed on is the answer to this gap.

**4.7 The local-language gap.** NZ-specific register barely appears in NZ agency copy. Most NZ marketing sites read like North American agency copy lightly translated. Real Kiwi voice (sorted, on the tools, up the road, properly, ringing) is almost absent. This is a brand-voice opening, not a positioning opening, but it's significant because it's the first thing that signals "this person actually sat in a small NZ business owner's kitchen."

## 5. Top competitors

Tiered by directness of competition rather than size, because size is a poor proxy for who Latetwenties actually competes with.

### 5.1 Tier A: Direct positioning competitors

These are agencies whose ICP overlaps significantly with Latetwenties. Same audience, similar price point, similar promise structure. The ones whose copy and positioning Latetwenties needs to read as *meaningfully different from*, not just better than.

**Four Stripes** (Auckland)
*fourstripes.co.nz*

Closest direct competitor on positioning. East Tamaki agency, NZ tradies and local service businesses, lead-generation framing, no lock-in contracts, claims $13.6M in client revenue generated. Their trademark "First Page, First Call™" system bundles website, Local SEO, and Google Ads into a 90-day rollout. Voice is performance-marketing-led ("get found, get hired, get busy"), not editorial. Visual register is conventional digital-agency: shouty headlines, performance metrics in coloured cards, founder photos, results-led screenshots. Their content strategy includes self-listing at #1 in their own "Top 7 Digital Marketing Agencies NZ" article, a solid SEO play. *Half their site is broken and AI-generated; we're not chasing them on listicle SEO.*

**ConstructMediaNZ** (Auckland, NZ-wide)
*constructmedianz.com*

Trades-only positioning, retainers from $2,000/month plus ad spend (so similar pricing to Latetwenties at the entry point), 3-month initial commitment then month-to-month. Strong voice in the bully register: "MORE JOBS. LESS CHASING. REAL RESULTS." with shouty caps headlines, "Dominating Google" copy, 90-Day Results Guarantee. Built on Wix. Founder claims 8 years on-site experience in the trades industry, which is a real differentiator the site leans on heavily ("we come from the industry we serve"). The "What if I've been burned by an agency before?" FAQ is direct evidence of the burnt-prospect pattern in the category. Their angle is *credibility through industry insider status*. Latetwenties' angle is *credibility through manaakitanga and relational practice*. Both are responses to the same fear, executed differently.

**Trades Online** (Helensville, Auckland)
*tradesonline.co.nz*

Trades-only NZ agency. Less prominent than Four Stripes or ConstructMediaNZ but appears in third-party lists and competes in the same SEO surface. Plays in the same space.

**Tradiesocials** (Auckland)
*tradiesocials.com*

Solo operator, social-media-led. The "comes in gumboots if needed" line on their site is genuinely good and captures the warmth signal that bigger agencies miss. Smaller scale than Latetwenties' Foundations product, more of an ongoing social retainer offering. Worth flagging because their voice and pricing sit closer to where Latetwenties wants to land than the bigger names. They're not direct competition by service mix but they're competition by *register*.

### 5.2 Tier B: Adjacent / tier-up competitors

These are bigger or more established agencies that some Latetwenties prospects will encounter, especially the wary first-timers who Google "best digital marketing agency Auckland" and end up in directories. Latetwenties needs a clear answer to "why not them" without sounding defensive.

**Pure SEO** (Auckland, NZ + AU + global)
*pureseo.com*

The enterprise-tier comparator. 16+ years, Google Premier Partner (top 3% globally), offices in Auckland, Wellington, Melbourne, Manila. Service grid spans SEO/SEM/Paid Social/CRO/Content/Email/Analytics/Online Advertising. Hourly rates $250+ per TechBehemoths. Public-facing voice is "no jargon, transparent results" but the actual operating model is full-service-large-team. Trustpilot reviews include the classic "SEO takes time, you signed up on lowest possible package" agency-side response Brendan flagged as a category red flag. Pure SEO is not Latetwenties' direct competition (different price tier, different model) but they're often the comparator in prospects' minds. The "small Auckland agency" framing is the answer: same city, opposite operating model.

**First Digital** (Auckland)
*firstdigital.co.nz*

Auckland-based, 20+ years, full-service, "two decades of digital wins" framing. Voice leans hype ("supercharge", "unlock") and uses the exact words on Brendan's brand-rules avoid list. Strong on case studies featuring named NZ clients. Direct evidence of how the established Auckland tier sounds. Latetwenties competes against First Digital not by trying to match their scale but by being the *opposite* of their voice register.

**Webzilla** (NZ + AU)
*webzilla.com.au*

Google Premier Partner. SEO/SEM/Web/Maintenance bundled. NZ + AU coverage. Performance-marketing voice. Listed across third-party directories. Tier-up comparator similar to First Digital but more performance-led.

**Firefly Digital** (Auckland)
*fireflydigital.co.nz*

Worth flagging specifically because their pricing model is a positive structural reference. Fixed monthly management fee with ad spend going direct to Google or Meta from the client's account, no markups. 700+ clients claimed. This is the rare NZ agency that handles the "where does my money actually go" anxiety with a structural answer rather than a copy answer. Latetwenties' equivalent of this is showing pricing prominently and naming what's in scope per product.

### 5.3 Tier C: AU comparators (relevant for cross-Tasman work)

Brief mention because Latetwenties already serves AU (TMT in Gippsland) and the positioning specifically widens to NZ + AU.

**Digital Nomads HQ** (Sunshine Coast, AU)
Tradies-focused, "no fluff, no cookie-cutter packages" voice. Played the awards game (claims top 260 globally). Smaller scale than the NZ enterprise tier but well-organised in their niche.

**BeKonstructive** (Melbourne, AU)
B2B and service-based businesses, brand visibility focus, AI-search positioning. Slightly different ICP (more B2B-leaning than tradie-leaning) but appears alongside in NZ directory listings.

## 6. What's working in the category

Patterns the better competitors share, which Latetwenties should match where they're table-stakes and break from where they're tired.

**Match (table stakes):**

- *No lock-in contracts after an initial commitment.* Four Stripes, ConstructMediaNZ, and most well-positioned smaller agencies have moved away from 12-month contracts. The 3-month initial commitment with month-to-month thereafter is becoming category standard for agencies serious about results. Latetwenties' Presence Care should match this exactly: 3-month minimum, month-to-month after.

- *Trade or service-business specificity.* The agencies that are winning in this niche are the ones that name the niche. ConstructMediaNZ doesn't take e-commerce. Tradies Online doesn't take restaurants. The specificity earns trust. Latetwenties' "local service businesses" framing already does this. Worth keeping the discipline tight; don't let the site list industries you serve as a comprehensive feature, list them as the *only* thing you serve.

- *Real case studies with named clients and named results.* Pure SEO leads with HomePro, Bell & Co, Cloud Edge Wellington. Four Stripes leads with revenue claims. The agencies without case studies look amateurish next to the agencies with them. Boaz, Hooper, and (eventually) TMT need to be on the homepage with named outcomes.

- *Free initial offer as the CTA.* Free SEO check, free strategy call, free presence audit. The category has standardised on "no upfront commitment to start" as the entry point. Latetwenties' Free Presence Check is correctly calibrated here.

**Break from (tired patterns):**

- *Shouty headlines in all caps.* "MORE JOBS. LESS CHASING. REAL RESULTS." is the category default. So is "STOP LOSING CALLS." The bully copy register Brendan rejected is exactly this pattern. Latetwenties' editorial-restraint register is the differentiator here, not just an aesthetic preference.

- *Hype words on the avoid list.* "Supercharge", "unlock", "premium", "dominate", "leverage" are still everywhere in this category. First Digital's homepage uses three of them in the first scroll. Latetwenties' avoid list is the structural answer.

- *Performance-graph-led visual proof.* Most agency homepages lead with a screenshot of a Google Analytics chart going up and to the right. Pure SEO does this. Four Stripes does this. The pattern signals "we'll show you traffic" which is exactly the smoke-and-mirrors fear from the audience layer. Latetwenties should lead with case-study outcomes (rankings, calls, jobs) shown as editorial proof, not dashboard screenshots.

- *Stock photography of generic "happy tradies" or "professional team meeting".* Universal in the category. Latetwenties' real photography requirement (Brendan working, real Auckland streets, real client work) breaks this pattern by default.

- *FAQ accordions as the dominant conversion section.* Every site has them. They're often padded. Latetwenties can do FAQs but they should earn their place by answering real prospect questions, not category-default ones.

- *The "Why us" 4-card grid with icons and small text.* This is the Farmform pattern from the visual brief, dressed up in agency clothes. Banned.

## 7. Category visual sameness

What NZ/AU agency sites in this category actually look like, structurally. The brief should make these defaults impossible.

**Header pattern.** Logo top-left, horizontal nav (Services / Case Studies / About / Blog / Contact), prominent phone CTA top-right, sticky on scroll. Universal across Four Stripes, ConstructMediaNZ, Pure SEO, First Digital, Webzilla. Latetwenties can keep the structural pattern (it works for usability) but should differentiate through type treatment, scale, and the wordmark itself. The Lust serif wordmark with trailing period already does most of this work.

**Hero pattern.** Oversized headline (sans-serif), one-line sub-head, primary CTA button + secondary text link, illustration or stock photo to the right. The headline almost always uses bully or hype copy. Latetwenties' editorial two-beat reveal (typographic statement on cream ground, then evidence) is structurally different from the entire category.

**Services pattern.** Either a 3-card or 4-card grid (Pure SEO does 8 in a 4x2 grid) of service categories, each with an icon, a title, and one paragraph. Latetwenties has only two products which means the grid pattern doesn't apply. The Juanmora pinned-scroll pattern for products is structurally different from the category default in a way that signals "we don't do everything; we do these two things, properly."

**Case studies pattern.** Card grid with thumbnail image, client name, and one-line result claim. Pure SEO uses video thumbnails. Most use static screenshots. Latetwenties should use editorial treatment: long-form Boaz case study on the homepage with photographs and ranking screenshots embedded, Hooper as a tighter mid-page entry, real proof rather than card-grid summaries.

**Typography.** Sans-serif everywhere. Inter, Roboto, Open Sans, Poppins, Montserrat. No display faces. No serif. Pure SEO uses what looks like Roboto. ConstructMediaNZ uses a Wix system font. Four Stripes uses a generic sans. The category has zero editorial typographic personality. Lust as the heading face is the single most differentiating visual decision Latetwenties can make.

**Colour palette.** Blue and white is the default. Sometimes blue with an orange or yellow accent. ConstructMediaNZ uses dark blue with a purple-to-pink gradient accent that reads as Wix-default. Pure SEO is brand-blue. Four Stripes is green and white. The category has almost no use of cream, off-white, or warm neutrals. Latetwenties' black on cream is structurally different.

**Imagery.** Stock photography of "generic professional people in conference rooms", "tradie smiling at camera holding tools", "team high-fiving". Pure SEO uses video thumbnails of presumably real client interviews which is a small step up. ConstructMediaNZ uses generic abstract gradient images from Wix's library. Four Stripes uses screenshots and what look like stock-quality team photos. Latetwenties' real photography requirement breaks this default by definition.

**Trust badges.** Google Premier Partner, Meta Business Partner, awards logos. Pure SEO has a row of about 12 award logos on the homepage. Four Stripes claims "New Zealand Made" certification. Latetwenties' decision: quiet variant only. Small footer line with named credentials *when earned* (Google Partner if pursued, Meta Business Partner if relevant). No homepage trust-badge wall.

**Net visual sameness.** If we hide the logo on Pure SEO, Four Stripes, ConstructMediaNZ, First Digital, and Webzilla and put their homepages side by side, four of the five could be the same agency. Latetwenties' job, visually, is to look like an entirely different category of business. Editorial publication. Considered practice. Not "another digital marketing agency."

## 8. Agency trust signals

For a small Auckland agency, the trust signal layer is structurally different from the trade-specific certifications a TMT or Hooper would lean on. These are the things that build credibility for Latetwenties without overstating size or experience.

**Highest leverage:**

1. **Real case studies with named clients, named businesses, and verifiable outcomes.** Boaz with the map-pack screenshots and the Reece quote. Hooper with the "electrician Red Beach" #1 organic ranking screenshot. TMT once it goes live. These are the load-bearing trust assets and they should be treated editorially, not as cards.

2. **Named principal visibility.** Brendan by name, by photograph, by direct first-person section on About. The category convention is to hide the founder behind a "we" voice. Latetwenties' counterposition is to make the founder visible *as the operator who does the work*. This is the answer to the senior-person-does-the-work gap from section 4.5.

3. **Direct testimonial with name, business, and specifics.** Reece's quote, named, with Boaz Developments attribution. One strong testimonial is worth more than a carousel of vague ones. The category default is the carousel; Latetwenties' editorial treatment is the singular pull-quote.

4. **Pricing visibility.** Counterintuitive but high-leverage. The category hides pricing because they price by what they think you'll pay. Showing pricing is itself a trust signal: it says "we're not negotiating with ourselves, this is what it costs." Already locked into the brief at $2,500 Foundations and $1,200/month Presence Care.

5. **Specific rankings or results, not vague claims.** "From not showing to #2 in neighbouring towns on Google Maps in under two months" beats "We deliver results." Specificity is its own credential. Boaz, Hooper, and TMT all have these specifics ready.

**Mid-leverage:**

6. **Years operating, framed honestly.** Latetwenties is newish as an agency entity, but Brendan has 10+ years of agency-side experience. The framing "10+ years across agencies, startups, and corporates" is honest and earned; it doesn't claim Latetwenties has been around since 2014. The site needs to be careful here: don't claim 10+ years for the *agency*, claim 10+ years for the *operator*.

7. **Google reviews on the agency's own GMB.** Brendan is setting up the GMB. Reviews on it become a real-time trust signal. Worth requesting reviews from Reece (already given), Boaz, Hooper, and any other clients who would write one.

8. **A list of named clients.** Even without full case studies, a "Clients we've worked with" footer or About-page sidebar with named businesses (Boaz Developments, Hooper Electrical, TMT Concreting, Physio Plus, etc.) is a trust signal at a glance.

**Lower-leverage but worth playing if achievable:**

9. **Google Partner certification.** Achievable if Brendan runs a minimum threshold of Google Ads campaigns. Worth pursuing because it's a category-standard badge that costs only operational discipline.

10. **Specific certifications or partnerships.** Meta Business Partner, Shopify Partner, SEMrush Certified, etc. Only worth pursuing if they correspond to actual work Latetwenties does. Don't collect badges for vanity.

11. **Press mentions or awards.** Not a priority. The category over-relies on these. Latetwenties' editorial register doesn't need an award wall.

**Anti-signals (things to NOT do, even though competitors do):**

- Stock photography of "team in meeting room high-fiving"
- A team page showing 12 fictional team members when there's effectively one principal
- Vague claims like "we've helped 700+ businesses" without showing a single one specifically
- Award badge rows that look like template decoration
- Generic "5-star Google reviews" callouts without actual review screenshots

## 9. Platforms worth targeting

Where Latetwenties should show up, and at what priority. Feeds the off-site E-E-A-T strategy in positioning_synthesis section 11.

**Tier 1 (essential, do these first):**

- **Google Business Profile.** Setup in progress. Auckland HQ, NZ + AU service area. Real photos of work and Brendan working. Reviews requested from Reece, Boaz, Hooper. Posts updated weekly through Presence Care methodology. Highest-leverage platform because (a) it's free, (b) it's the platform Latetwenties sells, and (c) it's the dogfooding proof.

- **LinkedIn (Brendan's personal account).** Where the audience actually researches before booking calls. Owners look up the founder before the agency. Brendan posting in his own voice about specific small-business marketing decisions, lessons from client work, opinions on industry patterns. Not company-page posts. Personal brand serves as agency credibility surface. The architect found Brendan through relationship; future architects will find him through LinkedIn.

- **Own website's case studies and Resources hub.** The four existing blog posts migrate. The Boaz case study is a proper editorial feature. Hooper is a tighter mid-page entry. TMT joins when live.

**Tier 2 (do once Tier 1 is humming):**

- **Clutch.co.** Verified-review platform owners use to shortlist agencies. NZ-specific category exists. Worth getting on with a verified review from Reece and one or two others. Sponsored placements available but unnecessary at first.

- **Sortlist.** Project-brief marketplace where agencies pitch back. Lower-quality leads than Clutch but free to list. Worth being present.

- **DesignRush.** Similar to Clutch, ranking-led, NZ-specific category. Free listing tier exists.

**Tier 3 (passive, don't expend effort):**

- **GoodFirms, Semrush directory, Trustpilot, TechBehemoths.** Aggregator surfaces prospects sometimes encounter. Get listed if it's free and quick. Don't pay for placement.

- **Industry-specific directories** (e.g. NZ Tradies Find, builderscrack.co.nz adjacent listings). Skip unless we find one specifically used by the ICP.

**Worth deliberately not playing:**
