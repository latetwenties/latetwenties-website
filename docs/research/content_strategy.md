## 1. Voice (single source of truth)

The full voice specification. Every downstream stage reads voice rules from this section. Build_brief lifts these rules verbatim into the brief Claude Code consumes.

### Voice in one sentence

*A plain-spoken Kiwi marketing partner who treats your business with the same seriousness you treat it yourself.*

This is the line that anchors everything below. A writer asking "is this in voice?" should be able to read that sentence and answer.

### Personality traits (5)

Each tied to evidence from research substrate or direct call material.

1. **Plain-speaking.** No jargon, no marketing fluff, no industry shorthand that owners don't already use. Customer language layer 2.4 ("Used words I didn't understand to hide the fact that nothing was happening") shows what the inverse looks like and why it loses trust.

2. **Considered, never hyped.** Earned adjectives only. Confident without grandiosity. Pre-research signal from system prompt: "Heavy, confident, considered." The brand-rule avoid list is the operational expression of this trait.

3. **Warm but direct.** Manaakitanga as operating value translates to copy that respects the reader's time. Kindness shows through directness, not through padding. Owners are time-poor; copy that beats around the bush is itself a manaakitanga failure.

4. **Relational, not transactional.** Latetwenties speaks to the client as the person they are, not as a "lead" or a "conversion event". The "we treat your business like our own" core message is the relational frame; voice across all surfaces holds it.

5. **Quietly confident.** The bar is set by the work. Voice never has to argue for credibility because the work argues for it (case studies, named clients, real outcomes). When in doubt, say less and let proof do more.

### Words and phrases to use

Pulled directly from market_research customer language bank (section 2.5) and from established brand language. The list is the canon for prospect-facing copy.

**Outcome words (use freely):**
- "ringing" / "the phone ringing" (the real conversion metric)
- "calls" / "jobs" / "work" (use these instead of "leads")
- "found" / "chosen" (the canonical outcome phrase: "found and chosen")
- "showing up" (in maps, in search; the diagnostic frame's vocabulary)
- "sorted" (Kiwi register for "fixed properly", weight that "solved" doesn't carry)
- "properly" (one of the strongest words in the brand bank; carries craftsmanship signal)

**Relational words (use freely):**
- "treat your business like our own" (canonical core message phrasing)
- "the same seriousness you treat it yourself"
- "we'll have a look for you" (calmer than "we'll do an audit")
- "tell you straight" (existing About page language, retained)
- "no pitch"

**Audience-locating words (use freely):**
- "on the tools" (universal NZ/AU tradie register)
- "up the road" (better local marker than "in your area")
- "your patch" / "your area" (use "your patch" for warmer register)
- "neighbours" (when the context allows; the manaakitanga register)

**Quiet structural words (use freely):**
- "small" (calmly stated, not defensive: "small Auckland agency")
- "once, properly" (the Foundations promise)
- "keep the signals active" (Presence Care framing)
- "what's working / what isn't" (diagnostic register)

**Emphasis words (use sparingly):**
- "honestly" / "frankly" (only when actually being honest about something inconvenient; overuse hollows the word)
- "real" (real photography, real results, real businesses; precision word)
- "just" (Kiwi understatement: "we just sort it", "just pick up the phone")

### Words and phrases to avoid

The avoid list is **non-negotiable** and binding for every surface. It includes the standard offenders (which apply to every Latetwenties piece of copy) and brand-specific bans.

**Hype words (always banned):**
- supercharge, unlock, leverage, premium, game-changing
- elevate, transform, revolutionise, disrupt
- world-class, cutting-edge, next-level, best-in-class
- empower, enable (in marketing register; "enables" is fine in technical contexts)

**Marketing-fluff words (always banned):**
- professional (use "reliable" or "considered" or "qualified" instead)
- passionate, passion, passionately
- peace of mind
- quality workmanship
- trusted local
- bespoke (unless describing something genuinely custom; usually overused)
- holistic, comprehensive, end-to-end (jargon stand-ins for "we do all of it")
- solutions (in marketing register; "we solve X" is fine, "our solutions" is not)

**Bully copy (always banned):**
- dominate, dominating, dominance
- smash, crush, beat (your competitors)
- outrank (in customer-facing copy; fine in technical SEO context)
- destroy, demolish, obliterate

**Implicit-comparison framing (always banned):**
- "with the seriousness most agencies reserve for X"
- "unlike other agencies"
- "the agencies that get this wrong"
- Any sentence structure that requires a third party to do its work
- The relational frame is between Latetwenties and the client; competitors are not in the sentence

**Voice register banned:**
- Em dashes anywhere (deliberate avoidance; the en dash and full stop carry the work)
- Exclamation marks (very rare exception, never in primary copy)
- All-caps for emphasis (caps are reserved for headings if at all)
- Rhetorical questions designed to manipulate ("Are you tired of agencies that...")

**Industry / vertical traps (always banned):**
- "Marketing for builders Auckland" / similar industry-specific page targets
- "Marketing in Mt Eden" / similar suburb-specific page targets
- Any framing that turns the niche into a list of industries served

### Sentence rhythm

Mix of punchy declarations and measured explanations. The cadence varies by surface, but the rules hold across the site.

**Short sentences for assertions, claims, and pivots.**
*"We'll show you exactly what's costing you jobs. Whether you work with us or not."*
The full stop is the work. The period at the end of `latetwenties.` is the brand's commitment to this rhythm.

**Longer sentences for explanations and context.**
A longer sentence earns its length when it's actually doing explanatory work, not when it's padding. Compound sentences are fine; sentence fragments are fine. Run-on sentences and subordinate-clause cascades are not.

**Paragraphs that breathe.**
Two to four sentences per paragraph in body copy. Single-sentence paragraphs allowed and useful for emphasis. Long blocks of text are anti-brand.

**Avoid:**
- Walls of unbroken text (Farmform pattern)
- The marketing rhythm of three short questions stacked at the top of a section
- Sentences that contain three or more "and"s

### POV (point of view)

**First-person plural ("we") is the default voice for service and product copy.**
Latetwenties as the practice acting on the reader's behalf. "We sort the foundations." "We keep the signals active." This is the voice of the agency.

**First-person singular ("I", Brendan named) appears in three specific places:**
1. The signed letter section on the About page (a real piece of writing under Brendan's name, not a 3-line italicised footnote)
2. Named pull-quotes elsewhere on the site, attributed to Brendan
3. LinkedIn content (Brendan's personal account; the agency's company page is "we")

**Third-person ("Brendan") appears when:**
- Naming credentials in About-page sidebar facts
- Case study attribution where the work was done specifically by Brendan
- Trust signals in editorial register that mention the principal by name

**Second-person ("you") for the reader, always.**
Owners are addressed directly. Never "the client" in customer-facing copy. The reader is the protagonist.

The "we" / "I" rule is calibrated, not absolute. The previous version of the site over-applied "we" and the founder disappeared entirely. The corrected balance: "we" carries the practice, "I" surfaces on About where personhood matters.

### Emoji and hashtag policy

**Emoji.** None in primary copy (homepage, services, case studies, about). Acceptable in two specific places: GMB posts where they aid scannability of a list, and LinkedIn posts where they match Brendan's personal register. Use sparingly even in those contexts. No emoji in headlines, ever.

**Hashtags.** None on the website itself. On LinkedIn or Instagram where hashtags are functional, use sparingly: 2 to 4 per post maximum, location and category tags only (#Auckland, #LocalSEO, #Tradies), never personality tags (#qualitywork, #passionate, #trusted).

## 2. Content pillars

Themes every piece of content should ladder up to. Five pillars. Each tied to a specific finding from market_research and a specific opportunity in the category.

### Pillar 1: Visibility correction

*Making invisible loss visible.*

The screen-share moment as recurring brand thread. Every prospect loses work to someone they can see ranking above them; most don't know it's happening. Content that ladders to this pillar shows the diagnostic frame in action: where to look, what to type, how to read what you find.

**Research justification.** The Defected ICP pattern from market_research section 3 — RBM, PhysioPlus, the architect — all closed on the moment of seeing where they actually stood. Pillar content extends that mechanism beyond the sales call.

**Example topics:**
- How to check if your business is showing on Google Maps for your service
- The map pack: why three matters and where you actually rank
- Why your competitor up the road gets more calls than you (it's not the work)

### Pillar 2: The seriousness practice

*How taking your business seriously translates into the work.*

Manaakitanga in commercial register. Not lectures about the value, but evidence of how it shapes specific choices: the listening before the build, the photography over stock, the named principal over hidden team, the honest pricing over hidden quotes. Content that ladders here demonstrates the operating value through specifics, not assertion.

**Research justification.** The "treat your business like their own" core message and the senior-person-does-the-work gap (4.5). Owners hear "we care about your business" from every agency; pillar content shows what care actually looks like in practice.

**Example topics:**
- Why the person who pitches you should be the person doing the work
- What a real listening process looks like (and why most agencies skip it)
- The reason we show our pricing

### Pillar 3: Foundations vs ongoing

*The two-product story explained without sales pressure.*

Owners get confused about whether they need a one-off project, a monthly retainer, or both. Pillar content clarifies the architecture: when Foundations is enough, when Presence Care earns its place, what each one actually does. Educational not promotional.

**Research justification.** The Hooper pattern (Foundations only, no Presence Care, still ranking). The Wary First-Timers ICP needs permission to start with one and add the other later, not commit to everything at once.

**Example topics:**
- Foundations vs Presence Care: which one (or both) makes sense for your business
- Why some businesses don't need a monthly retainer (and how to tell)
- What "fix it once, properly" actually means

### Pillar 4: The Google Business Profile, properly understood

*GMB as the most under-used asset in local service business marketing.*

The existing blog content already covers this territory well. Pillar content reinforces and extends it. Most local service business owners treat GMB as a form they filled in once. Content here shifts that to "your storefront on the busiest road in town."

**Research justification.** Search behaviour shows high educational intent around "Google Business Profile management NZ", "how to rank on Google Maps", and the general GMB knowledge gap. Three of the four existing blog posts already target this pillar.

**Example topics:** (the existing blog posts already cover most of this)
- The four ranking factors most local businesses get wrong
- How to ask for reviews that actually move the needle
- What Google sees when it looks at your profile

### Pillar 5: The honest economics of small business marketing

*What things actually cost, what they actually deliver, what's worth doing.*

Pricing transparency extended into content. Owners are trying to budget marketing into businesses that are already capital-tight. Pillar content addresses the maths honestly: what a real Foundations project costs, what a real Presence Care month delivers, what's worth doing yourself versus paying for, when to stop spending and let what's working work.

**Research justification.** The smoke-and-mirrors fear from market_research 2.2. The lock-in fear. Owners want to be told what's worth their money, not sold on more of it.

**Example topics:**
- The real cost of a properly-built local service website
- When a $500/month SEO package is worse than spending nothing
- What you should spend on Google Ads (and when not to spend at all)

## 3. Customer language bank

Verbatim phrases from market_research, organised here for writers to mine directly. This section duplicates content from market_research sections 2.1-2.5 because content_strategy is the SSOT and writers should be able to work from this document alone.

### Questions owners are asking (use these as headings, FAQ entries, and conversational starts)

1. Where am I on the map for [my service] in [my suburb]?
2. Why am I getting fewer calls than [competitor up the road]?
3. How long until I see results?
4. What does the monthly fee actually cover?
5. Will you lock me into a long contract?
6. Can I see what you've done for businesses like mine?
7. How do I know if it's actually working?
8. Why do other agencies always need "more time" and "more budget"?
9. Do I really need a new website, or can you fix the one I have?
10. What happens to my Google profile and site if I stop paying you?
11. How much of my time will this take? I'm already on the tools.
12. Will you talk to me directly or hand me to an account manager?
13. Are you going to make me look like everyone else?
14. Can you get me ahead of [named competitor]?
15. Why did the last person I paid disappear after the first month?

### Objections and fears (address these in copy without naming them as fears)

- The smoke-and-mirrors fear: paying for activity disguised as outcomes
- The lock-in fear: contracts that don't perform but can't be exited
- The bait-and-switch fear: polished sales call, vague delivery
- The disappearing-after-the-start fear: great kickoff, then radio silence
- The "they'll make me look like someone else" fear: research-and-listening failure dressed up as design failure

### Phrases describing good vs bad providers (use as voice calibration markers)

| Good (echo this register) | Bad (avoid this register) |
|---|---|
| Got it right the first time | Talked a big game and delivered nothing |
| Talked to me, not at me | Used words I didn't understand |
| Showed me what was actually happening | Promised the world, delivered a template |
| The person on the call was the person doing the work | Disappeared after the first month |
| Didn't push services I didn't need | Charged me more than I earned in new business |
| Felt like they cared about my business | Made my site look like every other site |
| Made me feel like I wasn't being sold to | Wouldn't tell me what they were doing each month |
| Came in gumboots if needed | Tried to upsell me into ad spend when SEO didn't work |
| Treated my business with the seriousness I treat it with myself | Spent more on suits and offices than on the work |

### NZ-specific language worth using

- "On the tools" (every NZ/AU tradie understands this)
- "Up the road" (better local marker than "in your area")
- "Sorted" (Kiwi register for "fixed properly")
- "Ringing" (the conversion metric tradies actually care about)
- "Quietly" (matches manaakitanga register, contrasts with "dominate")
- "Properly" (carries craftsmanship signal; one of the strongest words)
- "Mate" (sparingly; warmth signal that pure-Auckland-corporate misses)
- "Work" / "jobs" / "calls" (not "leads")

## 4. GMB post direction

Brendan is setting up the Latetwenties GMB. Posts there are dogfooding (Latetwenties practising what it sells) and a real conversion surface for prospects who find the GMB before the website.

### Length

100–250 characters for the body. Long enough to land a thought; short enough to read on a phone in a glance.

### Tone calibration

Match the voice document above, with one specific adjustment: GMB is a *casual register* compared to the website. Where the website holds editorial restraint, GMB can be 10% warmer and 20% more direct. Think "the agency talking to neighbours" rather than "the agency presenting to a board."

### What every post should reference

- A specific concrete detail (a place, a job, a number, a screenshot)
- Either an outcome (a ranking change, a review milestone, a calls-up week) or a useful observation
- A reason it matters to a small business owner reading

### What to avoid

- Generic motivational content ("Mondays are for momentum!")
- Stock images of any kind
- Hashtags as filler
- Posts that say "we just helped a client" without specifics
- "Tip Tuesday" / "Wisdom Wednesday" / any branded weekly hooks
- Sales-pitch posts that don't earn the reader's attention first

### Example one-liner CTAs (in voice)

- *"Free presence check, no pitch. We'll have a look and tell you straight."*
- *"Type your service into Google. Add your suburb. Where are you?"*
- *"Sorted in two to four weeks. Then you keep the work coming."*
- *"Two products. One for getting found. One for staying found."*
- *"If your phone isn't ringing the way it should, we'll show you why."*

### Example opening lines (set the tone)

- *"A Boaz Developments update from this week."*
- *"Most local businesses we look at have one thing wrong with their Google profile that nobody told them about."*
- *"Quick observation from helping a Mt Eden cafe this week."*
- *"Three months ago, this builder wasn't on the map. Today he's at #2 in his neighbouring towns."*
- *"Reading reviews of other agencies has become a strange hobby."*

## 6. Messaging hierarchy

What to lead with, what to support with, what never to lead with. This section governs every primary-conversion surface (homepage, hero, ad headlines, GMB post lead lines, meta descriptions).

### Lead with

- The diagnostic frame (the screen-share moment codified): "Where are you on the map?" / "Type your service into Google. Add your suburb."
- The relational core message: "treat your business like our own" / "the same seriousness you treat it yourself"
- The outcome: "found and chosen by local customers"
- A specific, named result (Boaz, Hooper specifics) when in case-study or proof contexts

### Support with

- Pricing visibility ("Foundations from $2,500. Presence Care $1,200/month.")
- Named credentials (10+ years across agencies, startups, corporates)
- Reece testimonial in compressed pull-quote form
- The two-product clarity (Foundations + Presence Care, no other services)
- The honest practice statement (the "no pitch" frame for the Free Presence Check)

### Never lead with

- 10+ years experience as the headline claim (it's a quiet trust signal, not a billboard)
- "We're a small Auckland agency" framed as smallness pride (calmly stated as fact only)
- "We don't lock you into long contracts" (defensive register; appears as fact in How It Works, not as lead)
- Manaakitanga as a tagline (demonstrated, not announced; lives editorially on About)
- "We do everything" service breadth (Latetwenties does two products, named)
- Awards, badges, or certification claims (none currently held; even when earned, they're footer-only)

## 7. Topics and angles to avoid

What Latetwenties does not write about, talk about, or build content around.

**Theme bans (don't join the chorus):**
- "10 ways to grow your business" / any listicle that reads as content-mill output
- "How AI is changing marketing" (the category is exhausted; nothing original to add)
- Generic SEO strategy posts that aren't specifically calibrated to NZ local service businesses
- "Personal branding for tradies" / any content that talks down to the audience
- Trend-chasing content (latest Google update, latest algorithm change) unless it materially affects what local service businesses should do

**Tone bans:**
- Any content pitched as "the truth about X" or "X agencies don't want you to know" (conspiracy-marketing register)
- "Brutal honesty" as a brand position (the word "brutal" carries the wrong register; Latetwenties is honest, not brutal)
- Comparison content that ranks competitors
- Content that requires reading the agency's mind to understand ("our methodology," "our framework," "our approach to X")

**Format bans:**
- Infographics with statistics that don't add up
- Listicles longer than 7 items
- Quizzes ("What kind of business owner are you?")
- "Ultimate guide to X" content unless it actually is the ultimate guide and earns the framing

**Angle bans:**
- Any angle that requires Latetwenties to position against a specific competitor by name
- Any angle that requires writing in a register Brendan wouldn't use on a call
- Any angle that depends on hype words from the avoid list
